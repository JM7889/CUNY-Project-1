Research reflection · Fall 2026

CUNY and the City: What Three Visualization Platforms Taught Me

I have generally approached data through tables, calculations, and fairly simple charts. For research, a well-organized table or a pie chart is often enough to show what I need to know. I have also worked in environments where the presentation mattered as much as the analysis. At the time, I usually relied on PowerPoint to communicate the results. I did not spend much time building interactive dashboards.

I also tend to stay away from proprietary software when an open-source alternative is available. I prefer to know how a program works, retain control of my files, and be able to reproduce a result without depending on a particular vendor. That preference is not a judgment about the quality of commercial software. It simply reflects how I like to work.

One reason I wanted to take Data Visualization and Design was to understand Tableau, a widely used tool for presenting data. Tableau is used in business, finance, health care, education, and public administration, where people need to communicate findings to audiences who may not have the time or background to examine a dataset themselves. I wanted to understand what made it useful and, just as importantly, what alternatives might offer. For Project 1, I therefore tested Tableau against two open-source approaches: Apache Superset and Python with Plotly.

Narrowing the question

My original proposal looked at many kinds of 311 complaints around CUNY campuses. The feedback was fair: with hundreds of complaint categories, the subject was too broad to produce a clear argument in the time available. I narrowed the study to two related but distinct issues: blocked bicycle lanes and potholes. They concern the conditions of city streets, and both are relevant to people who travel around campuses by bicycle or scooter. I was particularly interested in whether reports clustered near certain CUNY locations and whether those patterns changed by month.

The revised research question became: How did reported blocked-bike-lane and pothole complaints vary near CUNY campus areas from 2020 through 2025, and what could a map and a monthly chart help us see? Alongside that question, I had a second, methodological question: Which visualization system would be most practical for producing, modifying, sharing, and reproducing the same kind of analysis?

I kept the two complaint types separate. A pothole report and a blocked-lane report do not describe the same condition. More importantly, a 311 report is a record of someone reporting a problem, not independent proof that the problem existed or that a street was unsafe. Differences between campus areas may reflect reporting behavior as well as street conditions.

Three ways of approaching the same task

Tableau was the starting point because it is the principal tool used in class and a familiar reference in professional data visualization. Its main appeal is that the user can connect a prepared file and build maps, line charts, and bar charts by moving fields into place. In the Tableau version, I arranged a complaint map, a citywide monthly trend, campus comparison bars, and filters in a single dashboard. The interface helped me understand why Tableau is useful in an office or classroom: someone can revise the chart and discuss what the audience sees without first writing an application.

Apache Superset takes a different approach. It is an open-source dashboard platform built around databases. It is especially relevant when a university, organization, or research group wants several people to consult consistent data through a browser. Superset can build charts and tables, but there is more work at the beginning: running the service, connecting PostgreSQL, preparing the tables, and checking that the database and dashboard use the same definitions. My Superset experiment also included a separate Leaflet street map, with campus and issue filters and month playback. That detailed map was a custom HTML page, not a native Superset chart. This distinction matters because the visual result came from several tools working together.

Python with Plotly was the third route. Strictly speaking, Python is a programming language rather than a dashboard platform. I used it to retrieve and prepare public data and to generate an interactive HTML visualization. Python and Plotly are commonly used in research and technical work because they allow the author to specify the calculations and appearance of a chart in code. My Python result included a map of report locations, a citywide trend, campus comparison bars, a campus table, and filters. Compared with Tableau, it required more attention to scripts but gave me more control over the complete process.

Keeping the comparison understandable

To make the exercise useful beyond my own computer, I prepared three illustrated instruction manuals, one for each approach. They follow the same basic sequence: obtain the data, organize files, build the graphics, test the controls, and save the result. The Tableau guide emphasizes clicking and dragging; the Python guide explains the commands and the code; the Superset guide explains the database, charts, and the additional map. My intention was that another student could follow a method rather than simply look at my finished dashboard. The manuals are also a way of finding missing steps. If an instruction is difficult to explain to a beginner, there may be an assumption in the workflow that I have not examined closely enough.

For a fair comparison, the ideal experiment would use exactly the same cleaned records, campus coordinates, distance rule, and dates in all three applications. I have not fully reached that standard. The Tableau and Python versions were designed around 26 CUNY reference locations and a 500-meter radius; the Superset teaching prototype used three example locations and a 1,000-meter radius. Because these circles cover different areas, their complaint totals should not be compared as though they were three measurements of the same population. I therefore treated this phase primarily as a comparison of workflows and capabilities, not as a controlled benchmark of output counts.

I compared the systems using practical questions: How difficult was the installation? How much data preparation was needed? How quickly could I produce the first useful chart? How easy was it to change the design? What did it take to publish the work or repeat the data collection? And could another person understand what I had done? These questions were more useful than judging the software only by which dashboard looked better.

What the work revealed

The finished Tableau dashboard showed how effectively a visual interface can organize several views on one page. The map answered where the selected complaints had been reported; the monthly lines offered historical context for New York City as a whole; the campus bars made location differences easy to scan. There was one important caution: the citywide trend covers a different geographic population from the campus map. Putting them together is useful, but the labels must make that distinction clear.

The Python dashboard produced a different kind of result. It was a standalone HTML page with a cleaner, more application-like layout, summary cards, a coordinate-based map, comparison charts, and a table. Its advantage was not that it proved more about street conditions; it was that the steps for collecting, checking, and presenting data could be kept together in scripts and repeated. Its limitation was that a map built from latitude and longitude points is not necessarily as informative as a detailed street map. A different design would be required for street-level navigation or route analysis.

The Superset work was the most complicated to set up, but it introduced a form of data visualization that I find useful for longer projects. Instead of treating each chart as a separate file, the process begins with a database that can support several views and potentially several users. The Leaflet map added a more detailed street background and playback controls, while Superset supplied the database-connected charts. This combination was flexible, but it also meant more things could go wrong and more components had to be maintained.

The technical comparison reinforced these impressions without providing a controlled speed test. The project files themselves were small: the reported packaged ZIPs were roughly 8.5 MB for Tableau, 1.4 MB for Python, and 6.8 MB for Superset. These figures mostly describe manuals and scripts, not complete installations or downloaded datasets. Planning estimates suggested about 5 GB of free storage for either Tableau or the Python exercise, against about 20 GB for the Docker-based Superset setup. Installation and development times were also estimates, not measurements recorded under identical conditions. It would be misleading to use them to claim that one application is a certain percentage faster.

Which tool I would use, and why

If I were teaching someone with no programming background how to turn an existing spreadsheet into an understandable visual story, I would begin with Tableau. It is also an obvious choice when a team needs to produce a polished presentation quickly and already works in a Tableau environment. It makes the relationship between the fields and the chart visible to the person building it.

For research involving repeated downloads, changing definitions, statistical checks, or larger amounts of data, I would rely on Python for the underlying work. Once the program is documented and tested, it can perform the same operations again and preserve a record of how the results were produced. That is especially valuable when someone may question a number and want to trace it back to the source.

For a continuing project used by several researchers, I would consider Superset. A central database can keep calculations and datasets in one place while different users examine the results through dashboards. It requires more technical preparation and maintenance, but that cost becomes more reasonable if the system is used repeatedly rather than only for one assignment.

Conclusion

This project changed my understanding of data visualization. I had thought of it mainly as a better way to present an analysis already completed in a table. I now see that the design of the visualization, the structure of the data, and the way a reader interacts with the results affect what questions can reasonably be asked. The instructor’s feedback was important here. Narrowing hundreds of complaint types to two made the work easier to interpret and gave the comparison a more useful focus.

I can see the purpose of all three approaches. Tableau helped me understand a common professional standard and the importance of making findings immediately readable. Python remains attractive because I can examine and repeat the underlying process. For my own way of working, however, I feel most comfortable with Superset. I like the idea of keeping the data in an open database and building several related views on top of it. I would still use Python to fetch and check the records, and I would not hesitate to use Tableau when presentation was the main priority. The lesson for me is not that one tool replaces the others, but that choosing a visualization platform should follow the purpose of the research, the audience, and the work required to maintain the result.
